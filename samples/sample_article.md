# The Cost of Convenience

Not until the pandemic had forced millions of employees out of their offices did companies discover how much of their success depended on the informal conversations that had always taken place in corridors and kitchens.

## Why Efficiency Is Not Enough

Economists have long assumed that productivity could be improved simply by removing friction. Yet the evidence, which has accumulated over two decades of workplace research, suggests something rather different. Organizations that strip away every unnecessary interaction frequently find that they have also stripped away the very exchanges from which innovation emerges. What matters most is not the speed of a process but the density of the connections that surround it.

There is no denying that remote work has reshaped how firms measure output. If managers had known how difficult it would be to assess distributed teams, they would probably have invested far more heavily in written documentation. Instead, many of them simply installed monitoring software, an approach that is both expensive and counterproductive.

## The Hidden Price

The costs of convenience are rarely visible on a balance sheet. Although a digital order takes seconds to place, the warehouse worker who fulfils it may be evaluated by an algorithm that never pauses. Given that these systems are designed to optimise for throughput rather than for human endurance, it is hardly surprising that turnover in such roles has risen sharply.

Hardly had the first automated scheduling tools been deployed when complaints began to mount. Not only did they ignore the preferences of staff, but they also made it almost impossible for anyone to plan a week in advance. Far from resolving the problem, the software deepened it.

## What Should Be Done

Policymakers should invest in prevention rather than remediation. The more transparent a system of scheduling becomes, the more likely workers are to accept it. It goes without saying that technology itself is not the enemy; the difficulty lies in deciding who gets to set its objectives.

Some scholars argue that the second half of this century will be defined not by the invention of new machines but by the negotiation of new limits. Only when we treat those limits as a design question rather than an afterthought can we begin to build workplaces that are both efficient and humane.
